Expandable explanation

Open any section for the mathematical reasoning behind the program.

Why does a 0/1 multiple always exist?
Consider the repunits 1, 11, 111, 1111, .... For the part of n that is coprime with 10, there are only finitely many possible remainders. By the pigeonhole principle, the sequence must eventually hit remainder 0. That gives a number made entirely of 1s that is divisible by that coprime part. Any factors of 2 or 5 can then be supplied by appending zeros.
Why do numbers ending in 1, 3, 7, or 9 need no zeros?
Such numbers are divisible by neither 2 nor 5, so gcd(n,10)=1. That means the entire input is the coprime part m, and no trailing zeros are needed. The program therefore searches directly for a number like 111...111 that is divisible by n.
What does “mod” mean?
a mod n means “the remainder after dividing a by n.” For example, 25 mod 7 = 4. The notation a ≡ b (mod n) means that a and b leave the same remainder when divided by n.
What is gcd?
gcd means greatest common divisor: the largest positive integer that divides both numbers. For example, gcd(12,18)=6. When gcd(n,10)=1, we say n and 10 are coprime.
Why is the remainder recurrence so useful?
If a repunit has remainder r, appending another 1 changes the number from R to 10R + 1. Therefore its new remainder is (10r + 1) mod n. The browser only needs to keep a remainder in memory rather than the entire gigantic repunit while it searches.
Why might a phone-number-sized input still take a long time?
The theorem guarantees that a solution exists, but it does not guarantee that the shortest repunit is small. In the worst case, the search can require a number of remainder steps comparable to the coprime part of n. A ten-digit input can therefore imply billions of possible steps. This page uses a configurable safety limit so it does not lock up your browser. Raising the limit searches farther without changing the math.
Why this construction works even when n ends in 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, or 8
Factor the input as n = 2ᵃ × 5ᵇ × m, where gcd(m,10)=1. Find a repunit R divisible by m. Then choose t = max(a,b). Since 10ᵗ = 2ᵗ × 5ᵗ, the number R × 10ᵗ contains only 1s followed by zeros and is divisible by every factor of n.

Note: this finds a proof-based 0/1 multiple, not necessarily the numerically smallest possible 0/1 multiple when trailing zeros are required. It is designed to mirror the number-theory argument and remain memory-efficient.
